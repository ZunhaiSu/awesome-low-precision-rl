\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Abstract:} 
Quantized rollouts can lower the cost of language-model reinforcement learning while changing the policy that generates training experience. Mismatch with the learner can then alter gradient estimates, clipping decisions, and later trajectories. This focused qualitative survey synthesizes twenty studies through a policy formulation that separates numerical execution, decoding, and staleness, while distinguishing conditional correction from response-level change of measure. It organizes interventions by their direct targets: shared computation, numerical fidelity, gradient estimation, update influence, and optimization schedules. Three relationships recur in the reviewed evidence: correction and mismatch evolve together; alignment gains depend on the computation actually shared; and signed update contributions expose effects hidden by average discrepancy. These relationships explain why numerical design and learning correction can be complementary, and why agreement or throughput alone does not establish efficient learning. We connect these mechanisms to information requirements, learning outcomes, and system costs, and identify open problems in diagnosis, composition, and stateful collection.
\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} quantized rollout; low-precision reinforcement learning; training--inference mismatch; importance sampling; quantization-aware training.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Scope}\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{Why Low-Precision Rollouts Complicate Policy Optimization}
In language-model reinforcement learning, generation determines the responses, rewards, and histories from which the next policy is learned. Quantizing the rollout engine changes this source of training data as well as its computational cost. A local numerical difference can alter a sampled token, the learning signal assigned to a response, and the parameter update that shapes later generation. The resulting training--rollout mismatch is a low-precision instance of training--inference mismatch (TIM), with consequences that extend beyond the accuracy of a single forward pass.

The practical objective is to learn a capable policy within a finite computational budget. Faster generation contributes to that objective through the quality of the data it supplies and the updates those data support. Numerical agreement, optimization stability, task performance, and runtime capture different parts of this relationship. Aggressive clipping, for example, can stabilize training while discarding useful signal; a more faithful sampler can preserve that signal while reducing the throughput gained from quantization. Their value depends on how efficiently the complete learning process improves the policy.

Existing methods intervene at different points in this process. FlashRL uses actual rollout probabilities to correct learning from a different sampling policy; Jet-RL coordinates learner and sampler precision flow; subsequent studies act on quantization, clipping, gradient estimation, and local update dynamics \citep{flashrl,jet}. These interventions often coexist within one system. Numerical components act on learner or sampler computation, including the interface between them; learning-side rules determine how the resulting samples influence the next policy. Component-level classification preserves these distinct roles within integrated systems.


Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy-map} organizes these components by the computation or update they change. Cross-category assignments preserve the distinct contributions of integrated methods; Appendix~\ref{app:corpus} summarizes the corresponding papers.

\subsection{Scope and Related Work}
This survey focuses on numerical and execution mismatch in LLM reinforcement learning, especially quantized rollout and related low-precision training configurations. It covers a higher-precision learner paired with a lower-precision sampler, as well as low precision on both sides. Weight, activation, and key--value (KV) cache quantization affect different computational interfaces. Policy staleness enters where parameter changes interact with those interfaces.

The twenty focal contributions comprise thirteen method or system studies, six formulation or general mismatch studies, and one exploration boundary case. These roles describe their use in the synthesis: MiniRL, for example, develops the formulation and also evaluates FP8 rollout \citep{minirl}. The literature snapshot is October 19, 2026. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} describes source selection and version handling.

Broader surveys address low-precision LLM training, rollout strategies, and reinforcement learning for reasoning models \citep{lowprecisionsurvey,rolloutsurvey,reasoningsurvey}. The distinctive concern here is the connection between actual sampling probabilities, numerical execution, and the update computed from sampled data. General post-training quantization, pretraining-only methods, and speculative-draft acceleration enter only where they clarify that connection.

The collection supports a focused qualitative synthesis of primary studies. Numerical format, architecture, adaptation, and collection protocol remain separate comparison axes. Table~\ref{tab:coverage} summarizes the represented regimes; Appendix~\ref{app:terms} defines recurring abbreviations.

\subsection{Perspective and Contributions}
Numerical fidelity and learning correction are coupled. A more faithful sampler changes the errors a correction encounters, while the update rule determines which errors affect learning. Their joint design determines which sample contributions reach the parameter update and the computation needed to preserve them.

We develop this perspective by connecting policy definitions, failure mechanisms, and method components. The formulation in Section~\ref{sec:form} identifies which distribution each probability ratio refers to; Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} traces discrepancies through estimation and optimization. This foundation supports the comparison of numerical and learning interventions in Sections~\ref{sec:numerical}--\ref{sec:learning}. Their shared dependencies explain the published findings and practical trade-offs synthesized in Section~\ref{sec:synthesis}, from which the open problems in Section~\ref{sec:open} follow.

\section{Problem Formulation and Taxonomy}\label{sec:form}
\subsection{Learner, Behavior, Proximal, and Reference Policies}
Let $x$ be a prompt, $y_{1:T}$ a response, and $h_t=(x,y_{<t})$ its prefix. A policy is specified by both a parameter state and the computation used to evaluate it. Write $p_\theta(a\mid h)$ for the current learner policy and $p_k(a\mid h)$ for the same learner-side path at sampling snapshot $\theta_k$.

The behavior policy $q_k(a\mid h)$ is the distribution that actually sampled the token. It includes the rollout engine, numerical representation, and sampling transformation. Temperature and vocabulary truncation therefore belong to its definition: a raw-model log-probability returned by an engine need not equal the probability after decoding transformations.

The collection protocol is part of the behavior law. Under deterministic execution and version schedules, the generated history determines the sampler state, including cache entries computed under earlier parameter versions. The subscript $k$ abbreviates that rollout path. Cache provenance identifies the parameter versions and numerical paths that produced retained cache entries; a checkpoint identifier alone cannot specify this state.

A proximal policy $p_{\mathrm{prox}}$ supplies the denominator of a policy-update constraint. A reference policy $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ supplies a regularization target. These are different roles: a frozen pre-update learner may be proximal, while a much earlier policy is the KL reference. Neither is automatically the policy that generated every token. PPO and GRPO provide familiar contexts for these distinctions \citep{ppo,grpo}; mismatch-aware formulations require them to remain explicit \citep{flashrl,minirl}.

\subsection{Execution Mismatch versus Policy Staleness}
At a fixed prefix and token with positive relevant probabilities,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
\frac{p_\theta(a\mid h)}{q_k(a\mid h)}=
\underbrace{\left(\frac{p_\theta(a\mid h)}{p_k(a\mid h)}\right)}_{\text{parameter change / lag}}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{\left(\frac{p_k(a\mid h)}{q_k(a\mid h)}\right)}_{\substack{\text{same-snapshot}\\\text{execution / decoding}}}.
\end{equation}
The first factor captures parameter change since collection, including asynchronous version lag; the factorization requires no independence assumption. The second compares execution at the sampling snapshot. It isolates numerical differences when decoding is matched and also includes decoding differences when temperature or top-$p$ filtering changes. The intermediate policy $p_k$ is a conceptual reference and need not be retained by the implementation. In partial rollout, a response can span collection boundaries, so $k=k(t)$ may vary across tokens. If caches survive synchronization, their earlier computations remain part of the behavior path, so reconstructing $q_k$ requires cache provenance or the realized sampling probabilities as well as the current checkpoint.

Figure~\ref{fig:policies} separates these snapshot comparisons from the two policy roles used by the loss. A proximal anchor and a KL reference can be chosen independently of the actual sampler.

Two limiting cases make the distinction concrete. At the sampling snapshot, the learner-side parameter-change factor is one while execution mismatch can remain. With matched execution and decoding, the second factor can instead be one while policy lag remains. A numerical intervention and a staleness correction therefore solve different parts of the same ratio.

Probability ratios also require support conditions. A token observed from $q_k$ has a defined denominator, but that alone does not establish that $q_k$ covers every action with positive target probability. Truncation sampling can violate this condition. Similarly, a token-level change of measure is not automatically a correction for the prefix distribution. At fixed $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:is}
\E_{a\sim q_k(\cdot\mid h)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(a\mid h)}{q_k(a\mid h)}f(a,h)\right]
=\E_{a\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid h)}[f(a,h)]
\end{equation}
holds under support and integrability assumptions. It says nothing by itself about whether the outer distribution over $h$ is the desired one. It also does not undo changes to group-relative advantages induced by collecting different responses.

For comparison, the full-response likelihood ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trajectory}
W_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{P_\theta(y\mid x)}{Q(y\mid x)}
=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)}{q_{k(t)}(y_t\mid h_t)}.
\end{equation}
Here $P_\theta$ and $Q$ are the induced response laws, and the denominator factors must be their actual conditional probabilities under the documented protocol. Unobserved execution randomness requires marginalizing that state or extending the likelihood to the corresponding execution events. The two response laws use a common stopping convention; an end-of-sequence decision is included when applicable. For a fixed behavior law and a parameter-independent reward $R$, the usual likelihood-ratio identity gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seqpg}
\nabla_\theta\E_{P_\theta}[R]
=\E_Q\!\left[W_\theta R\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_\theta(y_t,h_t)\right]
\end{equation}
under support, integrability, and differentiation assumptions. The product illustrates why exact response-level correction can have high variance; multiplying only the current token ratio into an arbitrary sampled advantage is a different estimator. Nor does Equation~\eqref{eq:seqpg} directly justify reusing group-normalized advantages computed under $Q$. Score Centering explicitly distinguishes sequence-level importance sampling from practical token-level corrections \citep{score}.


A two-step example in Figure~\ref{fig:prefixes} makes the distinction tangible. The second-token conditionals agree exactly, yet the first-token histories have different probabilities. A correction using only the second-token ratio therefore leaves that prefix imbalance, whereas the full-response ratio includes the earlier decision.

\subsection{A Component-Level Taxonomy of Interventions}
We group components by the object they directly modify. \textbf{A: numerical interventions} alter representation or execution. A1 matches computation between learner and sampler; A2 controls quantization error or preserves parameter updates. \textbf{B: learning interventions} alter how sampled data produce updates. B1 corrects gradient estimation; B2 controls the acceptance, magnitude, or direction of policy updates; B3 adapts the optimization schedule.

Classification follows the operation and its target, while secondary effects can cross class boundaries. Alignment may improve fidelity, and both estimator correction and update control can use multiplicative weights. B1 targets a distributional expectation; B2 regulates sample influence on the update. QaRL and QuRL therefore occupy several classes through coequal components. An adopted correction remains separate from a system's proposed contribution; discussion locations indicate expository placement. Direct low-bit evidence for B3 remains limited.

\subsection{Numerical Regimes and Contribution Roles}
The same intervention can operate under different W/A/KV precisions, dense or mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures, low-rank or full-parameter updates, and synchronous or partial collection. Native kernels and quantize--dequantize simulation also implement different numerical paths. In particular, ``FP4 training'' leaves open the precision of master weights, backward computation, and optimizer states.

These configuration axes also preserve attribution: integrating a correction into a quantized system is distinct from introducing that correction. A study's contribution role and the regimes tested in its experiments are recorded separately.

\section{Mechanisms: From Numerical Discrepancy to Learning Dynamics}\label{sec:mechanisms}
\subsection{Sources and Propagation of Execution Discrepancy}
Scale selection, rounding, accumulation, kernel choice, batch shape, and cached state can all alter a numerical policy's output distribution. Two paths using the same nominal format can consequently disagree. FP16-based analysis illustrates the role of precision selection, while R3 exposes the effect of discrete MoE routing decisions \citep{fp16,r3}. Routing disagreement can also arise independently of quantization.

Shared-prefix comparison holds the token history fixed and isolates differences between conditional distributions. Free-running comparison allows different samples to change the histories visited later. These settings expose different stages of propagation: a small conditional error can redirect generation, while a favorable shared-prefix average can conceal disagreement on histories reached by only one policy.

Discretization introduces a further asymmetry. An update can be visible in the learner's parameter space but fail to change the sampler's quantized representation. Conversely, a small change near a rounding boundary can abruptly change a codeword. QuRL studies the former through update-aware quantization; TRACE targets inconsistency in rounding outcomes \citep{qurl,trace}. The relevant object is therefore not only error magnitude, but its relationship to the evolving parameters and the discretization boundaries.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanisms} organizes the resulting learning effects into relative influence, expected direction, and update selection. These routes can coexist, which explains why one aggregate disagreement statistic can miss the active mechanism.

\subsection{Distorted Estimation and Update Decisions}
At an observed prefix, numerical disagreement can change an update through its weight, its expected direction, or the clipping branch it activates. Absolute probability error captures only part of this effect. Writing $p=q+\delta$ gives $p/q=1+\delta/q$: the same absolute perturbation has greater relative influence when $q$ is small. Tails and confidence-conditioned statistics therefore reveal structure that a mean absolute error can hide. CIS uses this confidence dependence in its correction design \citep{cis}.

Estimation can also acquire a systematic drift. For a normalized differentiable learner distribution, $\E_{a\sim p_\theta}[s_\theta(a,h)]=0$. Under behavior sampling, the corresponding expectation need not vanish. At fixed $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:drift}
\E_q[A s_\theta]
=\operatorname{Cov}_q(A,s_\theta)+\E_q[A]\E_q[s_\theta].
\end{equation}
The expectations are conditional on the prefix and include the sampled action and any random continuation used to form $A$. Centering advantages across a prompt group need not center them at each prefix. Score Centering uses this distinction to motivate an additive correction \citep{score}: it removes a first-moment drift term while leaving the behavior distribution itself unchanged.

Mismatch additionally affects decisions made by a surrogate objective. In a clipped policy-gradient objective, changing a ratio can change which branch is active and therefore whether a token contributes gradient. Thus, a perturbation can matter through a threshold crossing even when its absolute magnitude appears modest. The full-pipeline FP8 study isolates a failure involving excessive clipping of negative-advantage tokens \citep{fullfp8}. This is a different intervention target from merely decreasing the variance of a sampled importance weight.

The sign dependence can be seen without assuming a particular quantizer. Let
$\ell(r,A)=\min\{rA,\clip(r,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A\}$, with $0<\epsilon<1$ and fixed $A$. Away from the two boundaries,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clipbranch}
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial r}=
\begin{cases}
 A\,\mathbf{1}\{r<1+\epsilon\},& A>0,\\
 A\,\mathbf{1}\{r>1-\epsilon\},& A<0,\\
 0,&A=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus the active clipping side depends on the learning signal. For an illustrative objective that clips the combined learner--behavior ratio, a same-snapshot ratio of $0.7$ with $\epsilon=0.2$ already suppresses a negative-advantage token, even before a learner update. This is not the decoupled surrogate in Section~\ref{sec:b1}; the example explains why naming the clipped ratio is essential to interpreting a failure or a repair.

\subsection{Feedback across Optimization Steps}
The learning loop turns a local discrepancy into a dynamic process. Updates based on sampled responses change the learner; synchronization transfers those changes to the sampler; subsequent rollouts come from the updated numerical realization. Stability depends on this feedback, not solely on whether one frozen batch has a small error.

The magnitude of a learner--behavior gap alone does not determine how an update changes it. In a simplified local picture, reducing a learner probability that is already below the behavior probability can enlarge their log gap. ACRL and TRIAGE use update-direction information in their controls \citep{acrl,triage}. For a shared-parameter network, however, one token's update changes other probabilities too. The sign argument supplies local intuition; interactions through shared parameters determine the scope of any stability result.

Optimization scale adds another feedback channel. Beyond Precision studies the relationship between mismatch and optimization dynamics and proposes response-length-triggered learning-rate decay \citep{beyond}. This connects numerical observations to the scale of optimization, while leaving transfer of the schedule to aggressive FP4 execution open.

\subsection{Diagnosing the Effects of Mismatch}
Diagnosis begins with the comparison used to isolate an effect. Replacing an inference engine can simultaneously change arithmetic, batching, memory use, and generated trajectories. A resulting quality difference cannot automatically be assigned to the precision format. VeXact constructs a zero-mismatch reference to help separate execution mismatch from other training effects \citep{vexact}. It supplies a diagnostic counterfactual against which the effects of accelerated execution can be interpreted.

\section{Numerical Design and Execution Alignment}\label{sec:numerical}
Numerical design can act on the learner, the sampler, or the interface between them. Alignment brings their computations closer at a shared interface; fidelity control preserves information that a low-precision representation would otherwise lose. The distinction matters because two approximate paths can agree without either becoming more accurate relative to a higher-precision model.

\subsection{Aligning Learner and Rollout Computation}\label{sec:a1}
Jet-RL coordinates the precision flow used by learner and sampler \citep{jet}. Its unified FP8 design matches corresponding operators and quantization granularity across the endpoints. This makes the computation, rather than the format label, the interface of alignment.

QaRL implements such coupling through rollout-aligned quantization-aware training (QAT): the learner's forward pass reflects the representation used during generation \citep{qarl}. QUADS takes a deliberately asymmetric approach, fake-quantizing learner weights while retaining higher-precision learner activations \citep{quads}. Both designs bring part of the learner computation closer to the sampler, but QUADS leaves activation error to a separate rollout-side component. These designs illustrate selective alignment; uniform precision across the whole model is neither its definition nor a prerequisite. QAT emulation and native low-precision kernels nevertheless remain distinct numerical paths.

TRACE shares realized quantization information \citep{trace}. Compact rollout-side guidance informs learner-side FP4 rounding when a common quantizer specification still yields different decisions near a boundary. Its local rounding argument explains the benefit at that interface; full-network divergence also depends on downstream computation.

The shared information distinguishes these approaches: Jet-RL coordinates precision flow, aligned QAT emulates a representation, and TRACE communicates realized numerical decisions. Routing replay couples discrete MoE choices through a related principle \citep{r3}. More specific coupling introduces dependencies on token positions, modules, and parameter versions. Agreement of the complete policies still depends on the computation surrounding the shared interface.

\subsection{Controlling Quantization Error and Preserving Parameter Updates}\label{sec:a2}
Alignment alone does not ensure that the sampler can express the changes made by training. For an illustrative deterministic quantizer $Q$, an update can satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantupdate}
Q(w+\delta w)=Q(w),
\end{equation}
even when $\delta w\ne0$. Visibility depends on the distance to a quantization boundary and on scale behavior, as well as update magnitude. QuRL's Update-Aware Quantization uses invariant scaling to alter the relationship between quantization resolution and parameter updates \citep{qurl}. Its numerical contribution is therefore distinct from ACR, which modifies the clipping rule in Section~\ref{sec:b2}.

Figure~\ref{fig:quantization} shows a smaller update crossing a boundary while a larger one stays within its cell. Changing the scale moves these boundaries.

QUADS addresses another residual: activation error in the rollout path. Its Residual Activation Compensation complements learner-side weight QAT, allowing alignment and error reduction to operate on different parts of the system \citep{quads}. The component ablations are particularly useful here because they distinguish what weight alignment recovers from what activation compensation adds.

FP8-RL maintains numerical fidelity as the policy changes through blockwise W8A8 rollout, scale recalibration for FP8 attention and KV execution, and dynamic weight synchronization \citep{fp8rl}. It integrates these components with adopted TIS/MIS correction. Its v2 experiments also explore end-to-end FP8, so learner precision varies across the paper's experimental settings.

KV4-RL allocates fidelity across positions instead of raising precision uniformly \citep{kv4}. INT4 KV storage is supplemented by bounded INT8 protection for attention-sink and recent positions. Its retained TIS recipe makes the distinction between sampler fidelity and conditional correction especially clear: protecting the cache changes the data-generating computation even when the correction is held fixed. The experiments use colocated, quota-based partial collection, with generation and optimization alternating; responses may span collection boundaries.

These methods preserve different information needed by learning. Parameter rescaling protects update visibility, residual compensation reduces activation error, recalibration follows distributional change, and positional protection targets sensitive cache states. Their W/A/KV targets locate the computation on which each mechanism acts.

\subsection{Residual Mismatch and Limits of Alignment}
Alignment and fidelity constrain different comparisons: agreement between endpoints and faithfulness to a higher-precision computation. Shared weights can coexist with different activations, and a common quantizer with different surrounding kernels. Each result is therefore conditional on the shared interface, parameter snapshot, and input history.

Residual differences connect numerical design to learning correction. A more faithful sampler may still lag behind the learner, and conditional agreement at observed prefixes does not restore a response distribution changed by earlier decisions. A learning intervention addresses how these remaining differences affect estimation or the resulting update.

Maintaining a shared numerical interface requires information as well as arithmetic. Operator choices require coordination, QAT depends on matching quantizer semantics, and rounding guidance requires storage and correspondence with learner states \citep{jet,qarl,quads,trace}. Fidelity control instead uses calibration, compensation, or selective execution to preserve sensitive information \citep{qurl,fp8rl,kv4}. \section{Learning under Residual Mismatch}\label{sec:learning}
Residual mismatch enters learning through both estimation and optimization. Estimator correction changes how behavior samples represent a target expectation; update control regulates their influence; scheduling changes the scale of adaptation over time. These objectives lead to different trade-offs among bias, variance, progress, and stability.

\subsection{Correcting Gradient Estimation}\label{sec:b1}
\paragraph{Multiplicative correction and truncation.}
Importance sampling supplies a conditional change of measure under the assumptions of Equation~\eqref{eq:is}. Practical surrogate objectives usually distinguish the mismatch correction from the proximal update ratio. With frozen $p_{\mathrm{prox}}$ and recorded behavior $q$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tworatios}
c_t=\frac{p_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t\mid h_t)}{q(a_t\mid h_t)},\qquad
r_t(\theta)=\frac{p_\theta(a_t\mid h_t)}{p_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t\mid h_t)}.
\end{equation}
A representative decoupled surrogate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:surrogate}
\widehat L(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_t \sg(\bar c_t)
\min\!\left[r_t(\theta)A_t,\clip(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\right],
\end{equation}
where $\bar c_t$ is a possibly truncated correction, the sampled advantages are held fixed, and $\sg$ denotes no differentiation through the correction weight. The separation exposes two different operations: bounding the mismatch correction $c_t$ and clipping the proximal ratio $r_t$. Individual objectives differ in how they perform and combine these operations. An upper-truncated correction, for example, takes $\bar c_t=\min(c_t,C)$, whereas masking can discard selected ratios. Here $N$ counts valid sampled tokens. Response-level averaging and length normalization assign different relative weights, so the reduction rule is part of the estimator being compared.

FlashRL's truncated importance sampling (TIS) provides a basic bounded correction using actual rollout probabilities \citep{flashrl}. MiniRL exemplifies the mismatch-aware distinction between behavior and proximal policies underlying this notation \citep{minirl}. FP8-RL adopts token-level TIS and masked importance sampling (MIS) variants, while KV4-RL retains TIS alongside cache protection \citep{fp8rl,kv4}. The system studies show how the correction operates alongside other numerical components. Truncation bounds extreme influence while changing the expectation; recording an actual behavior denominator supplies the required probability semantics, but does not remove that truncation bias.

\paragraph{Adapting the amount and location of correction.}
Adaptive importance sampling (AIS) uses observable mismatch diagnostics to interpolate between uncorrected and fully importance-weighted gradients \citep{ais}. Its adaptive quantity is the estimator mixture, not a precision schedule. Calibrated importance sampling (CIS) instead derives a confidence-dependent importance-weight cap from a discrepancy representation \citep{cis}. These approaches differ in what they adapt: overall correction strength versus the location of truncation across token confidence. CIS is used here as general TIM evidence; its BF16 cross-engine experiments are not direct FP4 validation.

\paragraph{Additive score correction.}
Score Centering replaces the score at a prefix with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:center}
\widetilde s_\theta(a,h)=s_\theta(a,h)-\E_{b\sim q(\cdot\mid h)}[s_\theta(b,h)].
\end{equation}
With the exact expectation, its mean under $q$ vanishes and the drift term in Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} is removed \citep{score}. The remaining covariance is still measured under $q$, so this is not full recovery of an on-policy gradient. The reported implementation uses sampler top-$k$ log-probabilities and models the omitted tail using the learner distribution; its default is $k=128$ \citep{score}. The omitted mass therefore enters through a modeled tail rather than being discarded. The coefficients used to form the correction are detached, and its fidelity depends on that behavior reconstruction.

These corrections differ in their targets and information requirements. Ratio-based methods use sampled-action probabilities to adjust relative weights; score centering uses a behavior-weighted score expectation to remove conditional drift. Truncation and tail reconstruction approximate different objects. Their gradient interpretation also depends on the learner computation, including any surrogate gradient through a quantizer. These distinctions remain relevant when corrections are combined.

\subsection{Controlling the Influence of Policy Updates}\label{sec:b2}
\paragraph{Restoring or reshaping update constraints.}
QuRL's Adaptive Clipping Range (ACR) adjusts the update boundary in response to the relationship between proximal and behavior probabilities \citep{qurl}. QaRL's Trust-Band Policy Optimization (TBPO) combines negative-sample treatment with sequence-level trust-band control \citep{qarl}. The full-pipeline FP8 method first matches the lower clipping quantile on negative-advantage tokens to a BF16 reference, then adjusts the upper boundary to rebalance positive and negative contributions \citep{fullfp8}. Periodic reference passes amortize the calibration cost. All three act on the update constraint, but use different signals and aggregation levels. ``Calibration'' in the last method refers to objective boundaries, not quantizer scales.

The shared issue is which learning signal reaches the parameter update. A token-level boundary can restore a contribution lost to clipping, whereas a sequence-level trust band controls a whole response. Widening either region admits more updates, but their value depends on advantage sign and influence. The clipping count becomes informative when connected to those retained contributions.

\paragraph{Controlling disagreement through update direction.}
ACRL uses advantage-guided probability adjustment, with response-level discrepancy relative to a reference controlling token reweighting \citep{acrl}. It regulates how updates change disagreement. TRIAGE combines segment-local risk information, direction-selective gating, rebalancing, and bounded repair in native NVFP4 execution \citep{triage}. Both belong to B2 because their operative rule controls update influence. Their different risk signals and aggregation levels determine which contributions are admitted, suppressed, or repaired.

The granularity of control matters. A response-level statistic aggregates evidence across many tokens; a segment statistic can expose local concentration; a token rule is directly selective but may be sensitive to noise. Aggregation trades localization against statistical reliability. Finer granularity improves localization only when the resulting diagnostic remains reliable enough to guide the update.

\subsection{Adapting the Optimization Schedule}\label{sec:b3}
A third intervention changes the scale of learning over time. Beyond Precision motivates learning-rate scheduling through the evolution of optimization and mismatch \citep{beyond}. This is complementary to changing a numerical path or a token weight: it modifies the trajectory of parameter updates themselves.

Schedule adaptation remains less developed for direct low-bit settings than the estimator and update controls above. Its promise depends on preserving progress as well as stability: smaller steps can avoid divergence while delaying useful learning. Comparable quality within a comparable budget is therefore the relevant test of transfer to aggressive quantization.

\section{Synthesis of Findings and Practical Trade-offs}\label{sec:synthesis}
Within-study comparisons connect numerical execution to update behavior and learned-policy quality. They expose how closely the endpoints share computation, how samples enter the estimator, and which contributions survive optimization. These connections explain why numerical agreement, stability, and quality can respond differently to an intervention. Training cost determines whether the resulting benefit improves learning efficiency.

\subsection{Experimental Regimes}
Asymmetric learner--sampler precision exposes differences between numerical representations. Low precision on both sides makes the consistency of their execution paths and the resulting update especially salient. KV quantization acts on retained state, whereas weight and activation quantization alter other parts of computation. Low-rank adaptation and full-parameter RL also provide different degrees of freedom for responding to these changes.

AIS evaluates diffusion-based LLaDA alongside autoregressive Qwen models \citep{ais}. Its autoregressive experiments connect directly to the token-level formulation used here. Interpreting the denoising results requires likelihoods defined for that generation process, so those experiments broaden the empirical scope without validating the autoregressive factorization for diffusion.

Architecture and collection protocol further condition conclusions. MoE execution introduces discrete route choices; cache-heavy long responses expose different runtime bottlenecks from shorter generations; partial collection permits a response to contain tokens sampled under different parameter versions. A method evaluated in one of these regimes may motivate a hypothesis in another, but that transfer is not itself an experimental result.

\paragraph{Correction and mismatch evolve together.}
QuRL observes disagreement beyond an initially stable period, with ACR and UAQ addressing different changes in clipping and update visibility \citep{qurl}. AIS adapts the correction mixture, ACRL regulates discrepancy through update influence, and Score Centering removes a conditional estimator drift term \citep{ais,acrl,score}. The common feature is feedback: an altered update changes the policy and the mismatch encountered later. Early agreement and bounded weights describe only part of this process.

\paragraph{Alignment gains depend on the shared interface.}
Jet-RL couples precision flow; QaRL, QUADS, and TRACE couple representations or realized rounding decisions \citep{jet,qarl,quads,trace}. QUADS separates two effects: asymmetric weight QAT recovers much of the performance loss, and rollout activation compensation further narrows the gap. Its asymmetric-versus-symmetric comparison shows why nominal symmetry alone does not define effective alignment. FP8-RL adds calibration and synchronization for a changing low-precision policy \citep{fp8rl}. Transfer depends on preserving the interface responsible for the gain and its surrounding computation.

\paragraph{Signed update selection explains effects hidden by averages.}
The full-pipeline FP8 study connects excessive lower-side clipping of negative-advantage tokens to pathological training dynamics, whereas TRIAGE uses local and direction-dependent information to select interventions \citep{fullfp8,triage}. Both link stability to the signed signal retained after control. An aggregate clipping fraction or mean log-probability gap can hide that structure. TRIAGE's MoE routing-conditioned analysis also shows limits to token-wise directional predictiveness, distinguishing a population pattern from a reliable predictor for each token.

\subsection{Efficiency Gains and Implementation Costs}
An intervention's computational benefit depends on the work required for generation, learning, synchronization, and correction. For serialized phases, iteration time can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:time}
T_{\mathrm{iter}}=T_{\mathrm{rollout}}+T_{\mathrm{learner}}+T_{\mathrm{sync}}+
T_{\mathrm{quant/calib}}+T_{\mathrm{other}},
\end{equation}
where synchronization and quantization/calibration costs are separated from generation and learning. Accelerating one phase exposes the others. With overlapping execution, the relevant quantity is the critical path, so the phase times in Equation~\eqref{eq:time} cannot simply be added.

The serialized model also gives a simple bound on what a rollout improvement can achieve. If rollout occupies fraction $f$ of the baseline time, a rollout speedup $s$ and added work $hT_0$ give $T/T_0=(1-f)+f/s+h$, holding the other phases and workload fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:timing} illustrates the arithmetic. Overlap, changed response lengths, or changed concurrency require a different cost model.

KV4-RL illustrates the difference between generation and training gains. Over 400 iterations on Qwen3-8B-Base, it reports $1.80\times$ cumulative rollout speedup and $1.27\times$ cumulative full-iteration speedup over BF16 rollout \citep{kv4}. Time or compute to a specified quality level connects these measurements to the learning objective. Endpoint scores, iteration time, and rollout throughput supply different parts of the picture. The trajectory is needed to recover time-to-quality when an intervention changes response length, learning speed, or checkpoint selection.

\subsection{Evidence Limits and Coverage Gaps}
The strongest empirical support concerns mechanisms within the settings in which they were isolated. Model families, tasks, horizons, hardware, and numerical kernels vary across studies. Mathematical reasoning is a recurring evaluation setting, while interactive trajectories and broader task distributions are less directly covered. Transfer across precision formats likewise depends on scaling rules, higher-precision exceptions, and the surrounding kernels.

This synthesis inherits the scope of its curated sources and the implementation details they make available. Source-linked observations expose the basis of the comparisons, while unreported conditions remain unresolved. The collection supports neither an exhaustive account of the field nor independent replication of its empirical results.

Direct low-bit scheduling evidence remains limited, and stateful provenance is only partly characterized. Multi-turn agent and tool interaction lies outside the direct evidence synthesized here; diffusion results are contextual to the autoregressive analysis. Seed counts, uncertainty, negative results, and cross-hardware replication are not systematically extracted. These are limits of this synthesis, not claims of absence from the wider literature.


\section{Open Problems}\label{sec:open}
The remaining challenge is to predict when mismatch becomes harmful, how interventions interact, and which benefits transfer across systems and tasks.

\subsection{Predicting Harmful Mismatch}
Learning depends on where error occurs and which update it changes. Confidence-conditioned tails, signed clipping distortions, conditional drift, and directional concentration are candidate diagnostics with closer links to the update than mean disagreement. Their ability to predict instability or lost progress early enough to guide intervention remains unresolved.

Collapse, reduced progress per update, and failure to reach a quality threshold need not share a precursor. A mechanism-based diagnostic should remain predictive across models and correction rules. Lowering it without improving learning may instead reflect correlation, or suppression of useful signal alongside the measured risk.

\subsection{Composing Numerical and Learning Interventions}
Greater fidelity alters the discrepancy encountered by a correction; restrictive update rules can conceal the value of finer quantization by suppressing the affected contributions. A component's value therefore depends on its marginal effect on learning progress and cost after other interventions act.

Multiple masks, truncations, or trust bands can suppress the same contributions, making weak learning appear stable. Analysis of the joint estimator must identify its bias and variance, the contributions retained from positive and negative advantages, and the cost of reference passes or reconstructed expectations. This connects compatibility to quality gained per unit of computation.

\subsection{Precision, Staleness, and Long-Horizon Interaction}
Partial and asynchronous collection can make the sampling version token-dependent. A probability gap may then combine parameter change with execution mismatch, as Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp} makes explicit. Separating their effects requires information about snapshots, replay, or retained cache states that a production system may not store. The smallest behavior record sufficient for reliable correction remains a joint statistical and system question.

Long responses amplify this difficulty. An early numerical perturbation changes the prefixes visited later, even if conditional correction at each observed prefix is accurate. This suggests allocating precision according to position, layer, context, and training phase, with learning benefit as the objective. Local tensor error alone cannot identify where the extra fidelity is worth its cost.

Multi-turn interaction adds tool observations and environment transitions that shape later behavior. These may need to accompany cache provenance and sampling probabilities in the behavior record. Evidence from single-response generation leaves the effectiveness of the surveyed interventions in these settings open.

\subsection{Generalization and Comparable Evidence}
Transfer depends on behavior and execution semantics. Correction requires probabilities of actual sampling events; rounding guidance requires correspondence between numerical states. Pre-decoding probabilities and changed synchronization protocols can violate these requirements. Controlled comparisons across stacks are needed to establish which benefits survive changes in kernels, collection protocols, and correction recipes.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Quantized rollout places numerical approximation inside the learning loop: the data-generating policy affects both the current update and future trajectories. The surveyed interventions address distinct parts of this loop through computation alignment, numerical fidelity, estimator correction, update control, and schedule adaptation.

Numerical fidelity changes the error presented to the learner; correction changes its influence on the update. This interaction makes average agreement, short-term stability, and rollout throughput incomplete measures of effective training. The goal is policy quality at lower total computational cost. Predictive diagnostics and joint-estimator analysis can clarify when a combination achieves that goal across systems and collection regimes.

\section{Source Selection and Version Handling}\label{app:protocol}
\paragraph{Construction of this snapshot.}
The collection comprises twenty focal studies selected for their contribution to quantized rollout, low-precision RL, or mechanisms needed to interpret training--rollout mismatch. Primary papers, official publication pages, and arXiv records provide the source material. This is a curated narrative review; systematic database retrieval and exhaustive screening were outside its scope.

\paragraph{Inclusion and exclusion.}
A focal study contributes a direct quantized-rollout or low-precision RL intervention, a mechanism needed to interpret that setting, or a carefully identified boundary case. General quantization and RL background is cited selectively. Papers devoted only to low-precision pretraining, unrelated generation modalities, or alternative rollout acceleration do not become focal entries solely because they use the same precision format.

\paragraph{Versions and names.}
The catalog distinguishes first-publication dates, publication venues, and reviewed versions. QeRL, QuRL, and QaRL use formal publication records in the bibliography. Conference and preprint versions, aliases, and changes in presentation count as one contribution. Unpinned arXiv links are distinguished from locally reviewed versions, since a later revision may change the claims supported by a source.

\paragraph{Attribution of evidence.}
Method claims and observations are attributed to primary sources. The likelihood, drift, and clipping identities explain mechanisms under the stated assumptions; they are distinct from experimental findings. Figures and tables are original syntheses, and constructed examples are labeled separately from published measurements. The companion evidence collection links observations to reviewed sources.

\paragraph{Evidence roles and verification.}
Contribution role identifies relevance to the argument; evidence domain identifies the setting examined. Neither grades experimental quality. Source-linked observations retain their conditions and limitations. No independent reproduction was performed, and unextracted statistical or implementation fields remain unassessed. The catalog separates identified reviewed versions from version-unspecified records.

\section{Evidence, Assumptions, and Transfer}\label{app:checklist}
Transfer depends on the relationship between the sampled behavior and the computation used to learn from it. Parameter versions, numerical paths, decoding, and retained state determine the behavior law; learner, proximal, and reference policies define separate loss roles. A changed execution path can therefore alter the meaning of a correction even when the nominal precision format remains the same.

The residual approximation identifies what a result leaves open: prefix selection after conditional correction, bias from truncation, unmatched execution after alignment, or error in a reconstructed expectation. A published combination supports its tested estimator and regime. Generalization requires the assumptions responsible for that result to remain valid.
